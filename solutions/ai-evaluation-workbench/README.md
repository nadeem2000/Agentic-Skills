# AI Evaluation Workbench

## Human-Supervised Generative AI for Development Evaluation

## Purpose

This flagship solution demonstrates how evaluation firms, MEL teams, NGOs, INGOs, research organisations and independent evaluators can use generative AI across the development-evaluation lifecycle **without delegating evaluative judgment, methodology, evidence interpretation, ethics, safeguarding, confidentiality or accountability to the model**.

The objective is not to automate evaluation. It is to make recurring evaluation tasks more structured, traceable and reusable while protecting the professional responsibilities that remain inherently human.

> **Core principle:** AI can accelerate the mechanics of evaluation, but evaluative judgment, evidence interpretation, ethics, methodological decisions and accountability remain with the evaluation team.

## Target users

- evaluation firms and development consultancies;
- monitoring, evaluation and learning teams;
- NGOs and INGOs;
- donor-funded programme implementers;
- research institutions and learning partners;
- independent evaluators and evaluation team leads;
- programme teams commissioning or managing evaluations.

## The organisational problem

Development evaluations frequently require teams to interpret complex TORs, design an evaluation architecture, develop tools, review large document sets, analyse qualitative evidence, triangulate multiple sources, formulate findings and recommendations, and maintain consistency across reports and annexes.

Generative AI can support many of these activities. It can also create serious risks if it fabricates evidence, obscures provenance, overstates conclusions, mishandles sensitive respondent data, reproduces bias or creates false confidence in weak analysis.

The workbench therefore treats AI as a **human-supervised analytical and workflow assistant**, not as an evaluator.

## Evaluation lifecycle

### Stage 1 — TOR interpretation

**AI-supported tasks**

- extract evaluation purpose, scope, criteria and key questions;
- identify geography, stakeholders, programme period, deliverables and timeline;
- distinguish explicit requirements from implied methodological expectations;
- flag ambiguities, contradictions and missing information;
- structure clarification questions;
- create a source-linked requirements summary.

**Human responsibility**

- confirm the authoritative interpretation of the TOR;
- decide how ambiguities affect methodology, resourcing and risk;
- validate all material requirements against the original TOR;
- determine which questions require clarification from the client.

**Quality gate**

The original TOR remains the authoritative source. AI summaries are navigation aids, not substitutes for contractual requirements.

### Stage 2 — Evaluation architecture

**AI-supported tasks**

- structure an evaluation matrix linking questions, criteria, indicators, evidence sources, methods, respondents and analysis approaches;
- identify evidence gaps and over-reliance on single sources;
- compare proposed methods against TOR questions;
- identify questions without clear judgment criteria or evidence pathways;
- support a traceable question-to-evidence structure.

**Human responsibility**

- approve evaluation questions, indicators, judgment criteria, methods and data sources;
- decide whether the architecture is feasible and methodologically sound;
- determine how evaluation criteria will be interpreted in context.

### Stage 3 — Methodology and inception support

**AI-supported tasks**

- organise mixed-methods design options;
- structure sampling considerations supplied by the team;
- compare proposed methods against evaluation questions and operational constraints;
- draft sections on data quality, limitations, inclusion, ethics, safeguarding and work planning from approved inputs;
- identify inconsistencies between methods, sample plans and deliverables.

**Human responsibility**

- own methodological design and sampling strategy;
- determine feasibility, ethics and contextual appropriateness;
- approve the final inception approach;
- decide how limitations affect the strength of conclusions.

### Stage 4 — Data-collection tool development

**AI-supported tasks**

- draft KII, FGD, survey, observation and document-review tools from the approved evaluation matrix;
- map questions to evaluation questions and indicators;
- identify duplication, leading questions or missing coverage;
- support adaptation for stakeholder groups and languages;
- help maintain terminology consistency across instruments.

**Human responsibility**

- approve wording, sequencing and respondent burden;
- determine cultural fit and safeguarding implications;
- protect informed consent and ethical requirements;
- pilot and revise instruments before field use.

### Stage 5 — Evidence and document review

**AI-supported tasks**

- create document inventories;
- extract source-linked evidence;
- classify evidence by evaluation question, criterion or theme;
- identify contradictions, gaps and unresolved claims;
- build an evidence matrix;
- compare reported achievements against available documentation.

**Human responsibility**

- determine source relevance, reliability and evidentiary weight;
- resolve conflicting evidence;
- decide what evidence is sufficient to support a finding;
- verify any material claim before it enters the evaluation narrative.

### Stage 6 — Analysis and triangulation

**AI-supported tasks**

- assist with qualitative coding and thematic organisation;
- compare patterns across stakeholder groups;
- identify converging, diverging and missing evidence;
- summarise supplied quantitative outputs without inventing calculations;
- support triangulation matrices;
- flag claims supported by only one source or respondent type.

**Human responsibility**

- own coding-framework decisions and interpretation;
- determine statistical and qualitative significance where applicable;
- decide how conflicting evidence is weighted;
- determine whether evidence is sufficient for a finding;
- conduct evaluative reasoning and judgment.

### Stage 7 — Findings, conclusions and recommendations

**AI-supported tasks**

- structure source-linked finding statements;
- test whether conclusions logically follow from findings;
- distinguish findings from interpretations and recommendations;
- check whether recommendations are grounded in evidence;
- identify recommendations that are generic, unsupported or assigned to implausible actors;
- support prioritisation criteria supplied by the evaluation team.

**Human responsibility**

- approve every finding, conclusion and recommendation;
- decide materiality, strength of evidence and evaluative significance;
- determine recommendation feasibility and priority;
- ensure recommendations reflect context, mandate and stakeholder realities.

### Stage 8 — Report QA

**AI-supported tasks**

- check consistency among TOR, methodology, findings, conclusions, recommendations and annexes;
- check names, dates, numbers, terminology and cross-references;
- identify unsupported claims, duplication and contradictions;
- compare the report against contractual deliverables and reporting requirements;
- flag missing evidence references or unresolved limitations.

**Human responsibility**

- conduct final technical review and approval;
- confirm factual accuracy and professional judgment;
- ensure confidentiality and safeguarding requirements are met;
- decide whether the report is ready for client submission.

## AI vs Human Responsibility Matrix

| Evaluation stage | AI can support | Human responsibility remains |
|---|---|---|
| TOR interpretation | extraction, structuring, ambiguity flags | authoritative interpretation, clarification decisions |
| Evaluation architecture | matrix construction, alignment checks | questions, indicators, criteria, methods, feasibility |
| Methodology/inception | organising options, drafting approved inputs | design, sampling, ethics, limitations, final methodology |
| Tool development | first drafts, mapping, duplication checks | wording, ethics, cultural fit, piloting, approval |
| Evidence review | inventories, extraction, source-linked matrices | relevance, reliability, evidentiary weight, verification |
| Analysis/triangulation | coding assistance, pattern comparison, synthesis support | interpretation, weighting, statistical/evaluative judgment |
| Findings/conclusions/recommendations | structure, logic checks, evidence-link review | final findings, conclusions, priorities, recommendations |
| Report QA | consistency, completeness and contradiction checks | technical approval, accountability and submission readiness |

## Evidence provenance rules

The workbench should apply the following minimum rules:

1. **Every material finding must trace to identifiable evidence.**
2. **Model-generated text is never treated as a source.**
3. **Evidence extraction should retain document, dataset, transcript or other source identifiers.**
4. **Direct quotations require confirmation against the original source.**
5. **Contradictory evidence must be surfaced rather than silently averaged away.**
6. **Missing evidence must be labelled as a gap, not filled by inference.**
7. **Quantitative claims must trace to supplied or verified calculations and source data.**
8. **The evaluation team determines evidentiary weight and sufficiency.**
9. **Draft findings should visibly separate evidence, interpretation and judgment.**
10. **Final report claims remain the responsibility of the evaluation team.**

## Confidentiality, privacy, ethics and safeguarding

Evaluation data may include personally identifiable information, sensitive testimonies, safeguarding disclosures, health information, protection concerns, commercial information or politically sensitive material.

An organisational implementation should therefore define:

- which AI tools are approved for which data classes;
- what information must be removed, redacted or anonymised before processing;
- whether data may leave the organisation's controlled environment;
- retention and deletion expectations;
- access permissions;
- procedures for sensitive respondent information;
- escalation routes for safeguarding disclosures;
- human review requirements for consequential outputs;
- consent and ethical conditions affecting secondary use of data.

AI convenience does not override evaluation ethics, informed consent, contractual confidentiality or safeguarding responsibilities.

## Risk and Control Matrix

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Fabricated evidence or citations | Require source-linked evidence and verify material claims against originals |
| Overconfident synthesis | Require uncertainty labels, contradictory evidence review and evaluator approval |
| Weak provenance | Maintain evidence matrices with source identifiers and question/theme links |
| Confidentiality or privacy breach | Use approved tools, minimise data, redact/anonymise sensitive information and enforce access rules |
| Sensitive respondent data exposure | Apply explicit handling rules and prohibit uncontrolled model submission |
| Methodological overreach | Evaluators own design, sampling, judgment criteria and interpretation |
| Bias and exclusion | Review stakeholder coverage, disaggregation, language, accessibility and missing voices |
| Over-reliance on AI-generated coding | Treat coding suggestions as provisional and validate frameworks/patterns manually |
| Unsupported recommendations | Require recommendations to trace to findings, evidence and feasible responsible actors |
| Loss of evaluator accountability | Maintain named human approval points for every consequential stage |
| Hallucinated quantitative interpretation | Use supplied verified outputs and require manual/statistical review |
| False consistency | Actively search for contradictory sources and divergent stakeholder perspectives |

## What this solution can improve

Potential benefits include:

- faster first-pass interpretation of TORs and evaluation documentation;
- more systematic alignment between evaluation questions, methods and evidence;
- clearer evidence provenance;
- more structured document review and qualitative synthesis;
- earlier identification of evidence gaps and contradictions;
- more consistent links among findings, conclusions and recommendations;
- improved report-completeness and consistency checking;
- creation of reusable evaluation assets across assignments.

These are **potential benefits**. Actual improvements in time, quality, cost or consistency should be measured in organisational pilots rather than assumed.

## Suggested pilot

A practical pilot can use either:

- a **completed evaluation** with known outputs, allowing retrospective comparison; or
- a **low-risk live evaluation stage** where AI use is explicitly approved by the organisation and client conditions permit it.

### Recommended pilot scope

1. TOR interpretation;
2. evaluation matrix development;
3. document-review/evidence matrix;
4. one qualitative synthesis task;
5. report QA.

This scope is large enough to test value and risk controls without introducing AI into every evaluation activity at once.

### Suggested pilot metrics

Measure:

- time spent by workflow stage;
- number of factual or provenance corrections required;
- number of evidence gaps identified before reporting;
- proportion of AI-generated material requiring major rewrite;
- evaluator-rated usefulness;
- consistency issues found during QA;
- number of unsupported or weakly supported claims flagged;
- sensitive-data, privacy or safeguarding incidents;
- reusable evaluation assets created;
- points where AI output was rejected because human judgment differed.

## Engagement model

The Evaluation Workbench can be adapted to an organisation's existing evaluation, research or MEL process rather than imposed as a separate system.

A practical engagement may include:

1. **Workflow discovery** — map the organisation's current evaluation lifecycle, tools, approval points and pain points.
2. **AI-use-case prioritisation** — identify tasks with useful value, acceptable risk and appropriate data conditions.
3. **Workbench configuration** — develop organisation-specific instructions, templates, evidence rules and human gates.
4. **Pilot** — test the workflow on a completed or approved low-risk evaluation task.
5. **Quality review** — assess accuracy, provenance, usefulness, privacy and evaluator effort.
6. **Training and adoption** — train evaluators and MEL staff on appropriate use, limitations and escalation rules.
7. **Refinement and scale-up** — retain only the AI-assisted stages that demonstrate sufficient value and control.

This can be delivered as a focused pilot, evaluation-team advisory assignment, workflow-redesign engagement, custom AI-skill development assignment or responsible-AI capacity-building package.

## What this workbench does not do

This version does not claim to provide:

- autonomous evaluation;
- automated evaluative judgment;
- automated respondent engagement;
- uncontrolled analysis of confidential field data;
- automated statistical validation;
- replacement of evaluators or subject-matter experts;
- guaranteed time savings, quality gains or cost reductions.
