# AI Evaluation Workbench Design

## Purpose

Create a second flagship AI-integration solution for the Agentic-Skills portfolio that demonstrates how generative AI can support the full development-evaluation lifecycle while keeping evaluative judgment, methodological decisions, evidence interpretation, ethics, safeguarding, confidentiality and accountability with the evaluation team.

The solution must strengthen the portfolio at the intersection of **AI Integration + Research/Evaluation + International Development** and complement the existing AI Proposal & Bid Development Workflow.

## Positioning

**Working title:** AI Evaluation Workbench — Human-Supervised Generative AI for Development Evaluation

**Core message:** AI can accelerate the mechanics of evaluation, but evaluative judgment, evidence interpretation, ethics, methodological decisions and accountability remain with the evaluation team.

**Primary users:**
- evaluation firms and consultancies;
- MEL and research teams;
- NGOs and INGOs;
- donor-funded programme implementers;
- independent evaluators and evaluation team leads;
- research institutions and learning partners.

## Design Principles

1. **Process-led rather than tool-led.** The workbench must be organised around the evaluation lifecycle, not around generic AI capabilities.
2. **Human-supervised by default.** AI-supported tasks and human decisions must be clearly separated at every stage.
3. **Evidence provenance first.** Findings, conclusions and recommendations must trace to documented evidence; model-generated content is never treated as evidence.
4. **Methodological integrity.** AI may structure, compare and draft, but the evaluation team owns methodology, sampling logic, interpretation, triangulation and evaluative judgment.
5. **Confidentiality and safeguarding.** Sensitive data, respondent information and safeguarding concerns require proportionate controls and approved tools.
6. **No fabricated impact claims.** Portfolio benefits must be framed as potential until measured in a real pilot.
7. **Consistency with the existing flagship pattern.** The workbench should match the clarity, structure and commercial readability of the AI Proposal & Bid Development Workflow.

## Scope

The flagship will cover eight controlled stages across the evaluation lifecycle.

### Stage 1 — TOR Interpretation

AI-supported tasks:
- extract evaluation purpose, scope, criteria and key questions;
- identify geography, stakeholders, programme period, deliverables and timeline;
- distinguish explicit requirements from implied methodological expectations;
- flag ambiguities, contradictions and missing information;
- structure clarification questions.

Human responsibility:
- confirm the authoritative interpretation of the TOR;
- decide how ambiguities affect methodology, resourcing and risk;
- validate all material requirements against the original TOR.

### Stage 2 — Evaluation Architecture

AI-supported tasks:
- structure an evaluation matrix linking questions, criteria, indicators, evidence sources, methods, respondents and analysis approaches;
- identify evidence gaps and over-reliance on single sources;
- check alignment between TOR questions and proposed methods.

Human responsibility:
- approve evaluation questions, indicators, methods, data sources and judgment criteria;
- decide whether the proposed architecture is feasible and methodologically sound.

### Stage 3 — Methodology and Inception Support

AI-supported tasks:
- organise mixed-methods design options;
- structure sampling considerations supplied by the team;
- draft sections on data quality, limitations, ethics, inclusion, safeguarding and work planning;
- compare proposed methods against evaluation questions and constraints.

Human responsibility:
- own methodological design, sampling strategy, feasibility, ethics and final inception approach;
- adapt methods to context and available resources.

### Stage 4 — Data-Collection Tool Development

AI-supported tasks:
- draft KII, FGD, survey, observation and document-review tools from the approved evaluation matrix;
- map questions to evaluation questions and indicators;
- identify duplication, leading questions or missing coverage;
- support localisation and adaptation for respondent groups.

Human responsibility:
- approve wording, sequencing, respondent burden, cultural fit, safeguarding and ethical suitability;
- pilot and revise instruments before field use.

### Stage 5 — Evidence and Document Review

AI-supported tasks:
- create document inventories;
- extract source-linked evidence;
- classify evidence by evaluation question or theme;
- identify contradictions, gaps and unresolved claims;
- build an evidence matrix.

Human responsibility:
- determine source relevance, reliability and evidentiary weight;
- resolve conflicting evidence and decide what can support findings.

### Stage 6 — Analysis and Triangulation

AI-supported tasks:
- assist with qualitative coding and thematic organisation;
- compare patterns across stakeholder groups;
- summarise supplied quantitative outputs without inventing analysis;
- identify converging, diverging and missing evidence;
- support triangulation matrices.

Human responsibility:
- own coding framework decisions, interpretation, statistical judgment, triangulation and evaluative reasoning;
- determine whether evidence is sufficient for a finding.

### Stage 7 — Findings, Conclusions and Recommendations

AI-supported tasks:
- structure source-linked finding statements;
- test whether conclusions logically follow from findings;
- check whether recommendations are grounded in evidence and assigned to plausible actors;
- identify recommendations that are generic, unsupported or infeasible.

Human responsibility:
- approve all findings, conclusions and recommendations;
- decide materiality, strength of evidence, prioritisation and feasibility.

### Stage 8 — Report QA

AI-supported tasks:
- check consistency among TOR, methodology, findings, conclusions, recommendations and annexes;
- check names, dates, numbers, terminology and cross-references;
- identify unsupported claims, duplication and contradictions;
- compare the report against contractual deliverables and reporting requirements.

Human responsibility:
- conduct final technical review and approval;
- confirm factual accuracy, professional judgment and submission readiness.

## Required Portfolio Components

The flagship implementation must include:

1. `solutions/ai-evaluation-workbench/README.md` as the main public demonstration.
2. A clear text-based workflow map.
3. An **AI vs Human Responsibility Matrix** covering all eight stages.
4. A **Risk and Control Matrix** covering at minimum:
   - fabricated evidence or citations;
   - overconfident synthesis;
   - weak provenance;
   - confidentiality/privacy breaches;
   - sensitive respondent data;
   - methodological overreach;
   - bias and exclusion;
   - over-reliance on AI-generated coding;
   - unsupported recommendations;
   - loss of evaluator accountability.
5. Evidence-provenance rules.
6. Confidentiality, privacy, ethics and safeguarding controls.
7. Suggested organisational pilot design.
8. Pilot metrics that can be measured without implying pre-existing performance gains.
9. Links to relevant existing technical assets, including country context, official evidence, data quality, localisation and responsible AI where applicable.
10. A commercial engagement model for evaluation firms, NGOs, INGOs and MEL/research teams.
11. Contact/collaboration CTA consistent with the portfolio.

## Evidence and Claims Policy

The flagship must never imply that:
- AI output is verified evidence;
- AI determines findings autonomously;
- AI replaces evaluators or subject-matter experts;
- AI guarantees faster delivery, better quality or lower cost;
- a specific efficiency percentage, time saving or quality improvement has been achieved unless supported by measured pilot evidence.

Use phrasing such as **potential benefits**, **can support**, **may improve**, and **should be measured in a pilot** where relevant.

## Suggested Pilot

A practical pilot should use either:
- a completed evaluation with known outputs; or
- a low-risk live evaluation stage where AI use is explicitly approved.

Suggested pilot scope:
1. TOR interpretation;
2. evaluation matrix development;
3. document-review/evidence matrix;
4. one qualitative synthesis task;
5. report QA.

Suggested metrics:
- time spent by workflow stage;
- number of factual or provenance corrections required;
- number of evidence gaps identified before reporting;
- proportion of AI-generated material requiring major rewrite;
- evaluator-rated usefulness;
- consistency issues found during QA;
- sensitive-data, privacy or safeguarding incidents;
- reusable evaluation assets created.

## Success Criteria

The implementation is successful when:

1. A non-technical development-sector decision-maker can understand the workbench in under five minutes.
2. An evaluator can see exactly where AI is useful and where human judgment remains mandatory.
3. Every material AI-supported stage has a corresponding human-control point.
4. The workbench demonstrates credible organisational value without unverified impact claims.
5. The flagship links naturally to existing technical assets in the repository.
6. The root portfolio clearly presents two mature proof points: Proposal/Bid Development and Evaluation.
7. The documentation is self-contained, professional and suitable for sharing with evaluation firms, MEL teams, NGOs and INGOs.

## Out of Scope for This Version

This version will not build:
- a software application or web UI;
- automated ingestion pipelines;
- autonomous field-data collection;
- automated statistical analysis engines;
- dashboards;
- vector databases or retrieval infrastructure;
- integrations with proprietary evaluation platforms;
- claims of autonomous evaluation or automated decision-making.

These may become future demonstrations after the workflow concept is validated with real users.
